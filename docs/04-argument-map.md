## Candidate thesis 1: "Ownership without agency"

**Claim.** Indian law lets a person under 18 own the things a business is made of (a share of partnership profits, shares, copyright, a trade mark, a bank balance, taxable income of his own) but gives him almost no lawful way to sign for them, and the adult who signs in his place has narrower powers than people assume.

- **Supporting sources**
  - Owning: Partnership Act s. 30(2) (India B1); Copyright Act s. 17 and Trade Marks Act s. 18 (India G); RBI circular (India E); Income-tax s. 64(1A) proviso (India F).
  - Not signing: Contract Act ss. 10-11 (India A1); Mathai Mathai (India A4); Suraj Narain (India A6).
  - The adult's limits: s. 183 and Raj Rani (India A1, A10); HMGA s. 8(1) "in no case bind the minor by a personal covenant" (India A11); Mir Sarwarjan and Subrahmanyam (India A10).
  - The odd exceptions that show the pattern: s. 184 agent, NI Act s. 26 cheques (India A1, A12).
- **What is new in it.** The framing across business forms. The contract-law half is already in Swaminathan and Surana (Prior work 1).
- **Strongest objection.** "This is the system working as designed." Guardianship is the legal answer to incapacity; the law lets the minor benefit and makes an adult responsible. Describing that as a gap assumes minors ought to run businesses, which is a policy view the sources do not establish.
- **Second objection.** Much of the "cannot sign" side rests on inference where no case was found (LLP partner, agent for an entity, IP licence). A reviewer can say the paper states law that no court has stated.
- **Possible reply.** Show that the guardian route itself fails on the texts (item 4 of the gaps list), so the design does not deliver what it promises; and label every inference as an inference.
- **Evidence strength.** Strong for contract, partnership and guardians (Supreme Court and Privy Council). Moderate for companies. Weak for LLP and IP (text plus inference).

## Candidate thesis 2: "India is the outlier in consequence, not in age"

**Claim.** All four systems draw the main line at 18 (Singapore for contracts). They differ in what happens below it. England, the US states examined and Singapore make the minor's contract voidable, so the minor may enforce it and the adult is bound. India makes it void, so nobody can enforce it, including the minor. For a founder that difference matters more than the age.

- **Supporting sources**
  - India: Mohori Bibee via Mathai Mathai (India A3-A4); Raj Rani, where the minor lost her own claim (India A10); Law Commission 13th Report (India A9).
  - England: Proform (UK A4); Minors' Contracts Act 1987 ss. 2-3 (UK A2).
  - US: Cal. Fam. Code §§ 6700, 6710 (US A2).
  - Singapore: Civil Law Act s. 35 (Singapore A2).
- **Strongest objection.** It is not new. Swaminathan and Surana, Cheong et al. and Star and Dhankar (Prior work 1-3) have made this comparison. A paper built on it alone would be a summary of their work.
- **Second objection.** The English cases that establish "voidable" were not read (UK A5), and the Restatement was not read (US A1). The comparative half would be resting on one first-instance English judgment and one state's code.
- **Evidence strength.** Strong on the Indian side; adequate but thin on the others until the unread authorities are read.
- **Best use.** As the background section that sets up thesis 1 or 3, with full credit to the existing literature.

## Candidate thesis 3: "The Limited Liability Partnership Act forgot minors"

**Claim.** The Partnership Act 1932 contains a complete code for minors (s. 30). The LLP Act 2008, drafted 76 years later for a safer business form, contains none: s. 5 says "any individual" may be a partner and lists three disqualifications, none of them minority. A minor can therefore be admitted to the benefits of a firm with unlimited liability but has no defined status in the form with limited liability. The paper would propose a s. 30-type provision for LLPs.

- **Supporting sources**
  - LLP Act ss. 5, 7 (India C1); Partnership Act s. 30 (India B1); Dwarkadas Khetan and Shah Mohandas (India B2-B3); Contract Act s. 11 (India A1).
  - Comparison: UK LLP Act 2000 ss. 2, 4 are equally silent (UK D); Delaware LLC Act admits any "person" including a "custodian" (US B2); Singapore's regulator states 18 outright (Singapore C).
- **What is new in it.** No prior work was found on this point at all (Prior work, verdict).
- **Strongest objection.** "There is no gap; s. 11 of the Contract Act fills it." An LLP agreement is a contract, a minor cannot make one, so a minor cannot be a partner, and Parliament did not need to say so. The silence is drafting economy, not oversight.
- **Possible reply.** Then why did the 1932 Act need s. 30? Because without it the same reasoning would have shut minors out of partnership benefits too. The 2008 Act removed the benefit route without discussion. (Whether it was discussed is a factual point: the 2008 Bill's notes on clauses and the parliamentary committee report were **not read** and must be.)
- **Second objection.** Limited liability changes the policy. s. 30 exists to protect a minor from unlimited personal liability; in an LLP nobody has it, so the special status is unnecessary.
- **Possible reply.** That argues for letting minors in, not for leaving them unmentioned.
- **Third objection (factual risk).** Rules or an MCA FAQ may already answer the question. The conditions prescribed under s. 7(5) and the MCA FAQ were not read.
- **Evidence strength.** Strong on the statutory text. Depends on two unread documents (the Rules under s. 7(5); the legislative history).

## Candidate thesis 4: "A director's minimum age set by a web form"

**Claim.** The UK (16), Singapore (18) and New York (18) each state a director's minimum age in the statute. India's Companies Act states 21 for managing and whole-time directors and nothing for ordinary directors; the 18-year floor in practice appears to come from the identification-number process, not from Parliament. A rule that decides who may run a company should be in the Act.

- **Supporting sources.** Companies Act 2013 ss. 152(5), 153, 164, 196(3)(a) and DIN Rules r. 9 (India D4); UK s. 157 (UK B1); Singapore s. 145(2) (Singapore B); NY BCL § 701 and Delaware § 141(b) (US B1, B5); practitioner notes that give no section (Prior work 20-22).
- **Strongest objection.** It may be wrong. The age may be stated in the DIR-3 form, its instruction kit, or a rule that the keyword search missed. If so the thesis collapses into "the age is in subordinate legislation", which is ordinary.
- **Second objection.** Even with no age rule, s. 152(5) requires the director's consent and a director signs declarations; a minor's consent and declarations raise s. 11 problems. So the Act does exclude minors, indirectly.
- **Evidence strength.** Text-based and striking, but the single most fragile claim in the pack. Do not build the paper on it unless a company secretary or lawyer confirms where the 18-year rule lives.

- Reasons:
  - Thesis 3 is the only claim for which no prior work was found and for which the key texts are short enough to quote in full.

- **Options that fit thesis 3 directly:** A (primary), with B and G as supporting proposals.
- **Options that need no change to Contract Act s. 11:** A, B, E, I. These are the politically realistic ones and the paper should say so.
- **A point Sean must decide himself:** whether the paper argues that 16-year-olds *should* be able to found companies, or only that the law should say clearly whether they can. The second is easier to defend on documents alone.

## Counter-considerations the paper must face (policy, not doctrine)
- Protection: the void rule exists to stop adults exploiting children. Any reform must say how exploitation is still prevented. Scotland's "prejudicial transaction" test and California's court approval are the two answers found.
- Creditors and customers: who is liable when a minor-run business fails or harms someone.
- Child-labour law: not examined (India J). A reviewer will ask.
- Parents: reforms that empower the minor reduce parental control. The sources do not address whether that is desirable.
- Evidence of need: the paper is document-based, so it cannot show how many teenage founders exist or what they do in practice. It must not claim that the law "pushes teen founders into informal arrangements" (the scoping note's wording) because no source read supports an empirical claim. It can say the law leaves no formal route.
