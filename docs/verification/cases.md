## A. Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose (Privy Council, 4 March 1903)
- Citation on the copy: (1903) L.R. 30 I.A. 114; 7 C.W.N. 441; 30 M.I.A. 114 (Manupatra's header). Often also cited as I.L.R. 30 Cal 539. Judgment of Sir Ford North; coram Lords Macnaghten, Davey, Lindley, Sir Ford North, Sir A. Scoble, Sir A. Wilson.
- Source: Manupatra free Privy Council page (printed footer "27-08-2024 ... www.manupatra.com"), saved as `mohori_manupatra.txt` / `.pdf`. **Opened in full (secondary copy).** This closes the pack's "not read in original" gap at secondary level; the BAILII and CommonLII copies were not obtainable (the BAILII capture index lists the 1903 UKPC volume but the case page returned only a stub).
- Facts (paras 1-6): infant, aged under 21 (Indian age of majority at the time), mortgaged houses for Rs 20,000 (about Rs 10,500 actually advanced) to a money-lender whose attorney knew he was a minor, having been warned by his mother's letter. Declared void and cancelled by the courts below.
- Holdings, verbatim:
  - Para 9 (estoppel): "There can be no estoppel where the truth of the matter is known to both parties, and their Lordships hold, in accordance with English authorities, that a false representation, made to a person who knows it to be false, is not such a fraud as to take away the privilege of infancy".
  - Para 13 (why the Board re-argued the point): "The general current of decision in India certainly is that ever since the passing of the Indian Contract Act (IX of 1872) the contracts of infants are voidable only. This conclusion, however, has not been arrived at without vigorous protests by various Judges ... their Lordships consider themselves at liberty to act on their own view of the law as declared by the Contract Act".
  - Para 16 (the core): "Looking at these sections their Lordships are satisfied that the Act makes it essential that all contracting parties should be 'competent to contract,' and expressly provides that a person who by reason of infancy is incompetent to contract cannot make a contract within the meaning of the Act."
  - Para 16 (necessaries): "it is clear from the Act that he is not to be liable even for necessaries, and that no demand in respect thereof is enforceable against him by law, though a statutory claim is created against his property."
  - Para 16 (agency and partnership): "Under secs. 183 and 184 no person under the age of majority can employ or be an agent. Again under secs. 247 and 248, although a person under majority may be admitted to the benefits of a partnership, he cannot be made personally liable for any of its obligations".
  - Para 16 (void / voidable): "The question whether a contract is void or voidable presupposes the existence of a contract within the meaning of the Act, and cannot arise in the case of an infant. Their Lordships are therefore of opinion that in the present case there is not any such voidable contract as is dealt with in sec. 64."
  - Para 17 (s. 65): the section "starts from the basis of there being an agreement or contract between competent parties; and has no application to a case in which there never was, and never could have been, any contract."
  - Para 18: "the Act, so far as it goes, is exhaustive and imperative; and does provide in clear language that an infant is not a person competent to bind himself by a contract of this description."
  - Para 19 (restitution under the Specific Relief Act 1877 ss. 38, 41): discretion exercised against requiring repayment "of money advanced to him with full knowledge of his infancy".
  - Para 20: Lord Justice Romer in Thurstan v Nottingham Permanent Benefit Building Society quoted: "a Court of Equity cannot say that it is equitable to compel a person to pay any moneys in respect of a transaction which as against that person the Legislature has declared to be void."
  - Para 21: "although there are no doubt decisions of some weight that before the Indian Contract Act an infant's contract was voidable only, in accordance with English law as it then stood." The Board also noted the result "seems to be in accordance with the old Hindu Law as declared in the laws of Manu".
- Points for the paper: (i) the Board's reading of ss. 183-184 is broader than the section's text (see sources-official.md, s. 184); (ii) the rule was decided on a mortgage by a minor against a lender who knew; the extension to every founder act is later case law; (iii) ss. 247-248 of the Contract Act (which the Board relied on for partnership) were later repealed and replaced by Partnership Act s. 30.

## B. Mathai Mathai v Joseph Mary @ Marykkutty Joseph (Supreme Court of India, 25 April 2014)
- Citation on the copy: AIR 2014 SC 2277; (2015) 5 SCC 622; 2014 (6) SCALE 41. Civil Appeal No. 4479 of 2007. Bench: V. Gopala Gowda and Gyan Sudha Misra JJ (Gowda J wrote). Source: Indian Kanoon doc 173680209, saved as `ik_173680209.txt`. **Opened in full (secondary copy).** The pack's unverified SCC citation is now confirmed by the copy's own header (still verify in the printed report).
- Facts (paras 2-5, 9): a Kerala deemed-tenancy claim resting on a 1909-10 mortgage deed in favour of the appellant's mother, "the age of the mortgagee ... was 15 years as mentioned in the mortgage deed itself."
- Holdings, verbatim:
  - Para 9: "To acquire the competency to enter into a contract ... the parties should have been of age of majority as required under Section 11 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872." The Court quotes Mohori Bibee (paras of "competent to contract" and "void or voidable presupposes the existence of a contract") and says: "Thus, it was held that a minor cannot be a contracting party, as a minor is not competent to contract as per Section 11 of the Indian Contract Act."
  - Para 9: the mortgage was "not a valid mortgage deed ... for the reason that the deceased mother at the time of execution and registration of the document was a minor, aged 15 years, and she was not represented by her natural guardian to constitute the document as valid as she has not attained majority according to law."
  - Para 9 (disapproving the older line): "Many courts have held that a minor can be a mortgagee as it is transfer of property in the interest of the minor. We feel that this is an erroneous application of the law keeping in mind the decision of the Privy Council in Mohori Bibee's case".
  - Para 10: "As per the Indian Contract Act, 1872 it is clearly stated that for an agreement to become a contract, the parties must be competent to contract, wherein age of majority is a condition for competency. A deed of mortgage is a contract and we cannot hold that a mortgage in the name of a minor is valid, simply because it is in the interests of the minor unless she is represented by her natural guardian or guardian appointed by the court. The law cannot be read differently for a minor who is a mortgagor and a minor who is a mortgagee ... Therefore, this Court has to hold that the mortgage deed-Ex.A1 is void ab initio in law and the appellant cannot claim any rights under it."
- Points for the paper: the "unless she is represented by her natural guardian or guardian appointed by the court" clause is the Supreme Court's own opening for guardian-led contracting; Mathai Mathai does not discuss HMGA s. 8 or the earlier Full Bench (Raghava Chariar) by name in the passages read. It is a two-judge bench decision.

## C. Raj Rani v Prem Adib (Bombay High Court, Desai J, 21 July 1948)
- Citation on the copy: AIR 1949 Bom 215; (1949) 51 BomLR 256. Source: Indian Kanoon doc 1972437, saved as `ik_1972437.txt`. **Opened in full (secondary copy)** for the paragraphs quoted (paras 1-15, 27-35); the final order was not separately read.
- Facts (paras 2-9): a minor actress, suing by her father as next friend, claimed damages for breach of a film-service contract made by her father "for and on her behalf"; the Court tried as a preliminary issue whether she could maintain the suit.
- Holdings, verbatim:
  - Para 10: "under Section 183 of the Indian Contract Act, a minor is not entitled to employ an agent. The contract, therefore, though it is made for and on behalf of the minor by a person who purported to act as his agent, is not the contract of the minor. In fact under Section 11 of the Contract Act a minor cannot enter into a contract. Therefore, in order that there should be a contract, it must be a contract entered into with the guardian of the minor by the other party to the contract."
  - Para 12: "Under Section 11 the plaintiff was not competent to enter into a contract and, therefore, her promise would not be enforceable against her. In my opinion, therefore, the plaintiff's promise to serve supplies no consideration and the contract was therefore void."
  - Para 14: "the plaintiff's promise did not constitute any consideration in law and therefore there was in law no contract."
  - Para 29: "I realize that as a result of this judgment minors may lose the benefit of contracts of service which have been considered so beneficial to them as to be put in the category of necessaries. I am, however, not concerned with the policy of the Legislature under which all contracts of minors were made void and therefore unenforceable by or against the minor."
  - Para 31: "As the minor's contract is a void contract, he is not entitled to sue for damages for breach of such contract including the contract of service where the contract was entered into by the minor himself."
  - Para 32: "But according to Indian law the contract is not binding on the minor, and, therefore, on the ground of want of mutuality, one should hesitate considerably before such a contract is held binding on the other side."
- Points for the paper: the nearest reported analogue to a parent signing a commercial contract "for" a teenager; reasoning rests on ss. 183 and 11 and on the minor's promise being no consideration. It left open (para 31) the line of cases where the minor has already fully performed (Raghava Chariar FB, Hanmant Lakshman, Abdul Ghaffar).

## D. Minors as members of companies

### D1. Diwan Singh v Minerva Films Ltd (Punjab High Court, 1958/59) - **passage only**
- The case itself was **not found in full text**. It is described in **CIT v T.P.S.H. Sokkalal (Madras High Court, N.V. Balasubramanian J, 29 April 1997, [1999] 236 ITR 981 (Mad))**, para 18, saved as `ik_1105217.txt`: "In Diwan Singh vs. Minerva Films Ltd. (1958) 28 Comp. Cas. 191 (Punj), the Punjab High Court held that there was no bar to a minor acquiring or holding shares in a joint stock company and when the shares were fully paid up, they were subject to no obligation. However, it is not necessary to pursue that aspect whether the minor can acquire or hold shares in his own name".
- Citation discrepancy to settle by hand: the pack gave "AIR 1959 Punj 106"; Sokkalal gives "(1958) 28 Comp. Cas. 191 (Punj)"; Daddy S. Mazda v K.R. Irani (Cal HC 1974, [1977] 47 Comp Cas 39 (Cal)) cites "Diwan Singh v. Minerva Films Ltd. [1959] 29 Comp Cas 263 (Punj)" but only for a point on summary rectification of the register (not minors). These may be one case in different reports or different stages. Do not cite a holding of Diwan Singh beyond the Sokkalal sentence until the report is read.
- Sokkalal's own holding was on income-tax: shares standing in the mother's name "on behalf of her minor sons" carry voting power and are counted for "substantial interest" (s. 2(32)); the Court expressly did not decide whether a minor can hold shares in his own name.

### D2. Master Gautam R. Padival (Minor) v Karnataka Theatres Ltd (Company Law Board, S. Balasubramanian, Vice-Chairman, 16 December 1999)
- Citation on the copy: [2000] 100 Comp Cas 124 (CLB). Source: Indian Kanoon doc 1302105, saved as `ik_1302105.txt`. **Opened in full (secondary copy).** This is the leading Company Law Board decision found on minors as members; it is the answer to the task's "CLB or NCLT decisions on minors as members".
- Question (para 1): "whether the name of a minor who has obtained shares by transfer, could be entered in the register of members of a company or not." Two shares bought by the father and lodged for transfer to the minor "represented by father and natural guardian, jointly with" the mother; the board refused on legal advice that s. 41 of the Companies Act 1956 needs the member to agree in writing and a minor cannot.
- Holdings, verbatim:
  - Para 5: "There is no specific provision in the Companies Act prohibiting a minor from becoming a member of a company. If at all there is any such prohibition, it is only an implied prohibition by virtue of the provisions of Section 41(2) read with Section 11 of the Indian Contract Act."
  - Para 6: the words "agrees in writing" were inserted in 1960 to protect innocent persons from being made contributories; "the provisions of Section 41(2) are not mandatory but only directory"; estoppel and waiver apply.
  - Para 7: "Assuming that in terms of Section 41(2) a minor cannot agree in writing to become a member, the settled law is that the natural guardian of a minor could enter into contracts on behalf of the minor for the latter's benefit and in such cases, such contracts are binding on the minor." The Board relied on Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act s. 8(1) and concluded: "Thus, it is clear that a guardian could agree in writing on behalf of a minor to become a member."
  - Para 7 (adopting R. Balaraman v Buckingham and Carnatic Co. Ltd. [1969] Comp LJ 82 (CLB)): "Since the shares sought to be transferred in the names of the minor daughters ... are fully paid-up shares, there will be no personal covenant to bind the minors".
  - Para 7 (policy): "Section 11 of the Contract Act is a beneficial provision enacted in favour of minors and we feel that this beneficial provision should not be used to their detriment. Therefore, we are of the view that there will be no bar in the shares registered in the name of a minor indicating therein the name of the guardian representing the minor."
  - Para 8: company directed to register the transfer "in the names of Master Gautam R. Padival (minor) represented by father and natural guardian".
- Also in the report (counsel's arguments, para 3, not holdings): a Department of Company Affairs "Departmental Letter No. 8/18(1)/63-PR, dated March 31, 1961" advising Registrars not to object to allotment or transfer of shares to a minor; Nandita Jain v Bennett and Colman Co. Ltd. (CLB Appeal No. 27 of 1972, 17 February 1998); Fazulbhoi Jaffer v Credit Bank of India (1914) AIR 1914 Bom 128. Contra counsel: Ram Kishan v Kanwar Papers (HP 1990) and Kumaran Potty v Venad Pharmaceuticals (Ker 1989). None of these was read.
- Scope: transfer of **fully paid** shares through a **natural guardian** under the 1956 Act. Not an allotment (the Palaniappa / Dalmia situation), not a partly paid share, and not under the 2013 Act.

### D3. Older High Court cases on allotment (read in full, secondary copy)
- **M.S. Palaniappa Mudaliar v Official Liquidator, Pasupathi Bank Ltd (Madras HC, Mockett J, 2 February 1942)**, (1942) 1 MLJ 425, AIR 1942 Mad 470; `ik_1090788.txt`. Para 2: "An application was made by M.S. Palaniappa Mudaliar as guardian of his minor daughter Meenakshi for shares and the company issued shares to and registered shares in the name of Meenakshi, describing her as a minor. This transaction was void on the face of it. An application for shares cannot be a necessity for a minor". The father was not liable as contributory in her place (liquidation context).
- **Dalmia Jain Airways Ltd v Saroj Rani (Punjab HC, Kapur J, 24 March 1954)**, AIR 1956 Punj 41; `ik_989434.txt`. Para 4: follows Palaniappa; where the father applies as guardian for shares for minor daughters and the company registers them as minors, "the transaction is void on the face of it and the father of the minor who has signed the application cannot be deemed to have contracted for the shares and cannot be placed on the list of contributories." Khosla J agreed.
- Reconciliation to state in the paper's own words: Palaniappa and Dalmia are about **applications for allotment** where the company later wanted money (liquidation); Padival is about **registering a transfer of fully paid shares** on a guardian's application. Whether Palaniappa survives for fully paid allotments under the 2013 Act was not found.

Opened only to identify the subject; the ruling was not read. Relevance: limits of a guardian's dealings with a minor's shares.

## E. Lovell and Christmas v Beauchamp [1894] AC 607 (House of Lords) - **passage only**
- The report itself is **not read** (not found on a free site). What follows is from the Calcutta High Court, which analysed it: **Sanyasi Charan Mandal v Asutosh Ghose (Cal HC, Mookerjee and Beachcroft JJ, 17 March 1914), (1915) ILR 42 Cal 225, AIR 1915 Cal 482**, paras 9-11, `ik_1726694.txt` (opened in full).
  - Para 9: "There is no room for controversy, as is clear from the decision of the House of Lords in Lovell & Christmas v. G. W. Beauchamp [1894] A. C. 607, that the District Judge correctly held that under the English Law, an infant partner cannot be adjudicated an insolvent."
  - Para 11 (facts as the Calcutta court gives them): Gilbert Walter Beauchamp "was a partner in the firm of Beauchamp and Brothers. He was an infant and the other partner was Ralph Beauchamp." Goods were supplied to the firm and not paid for; judgments against the firm were entered, but execution was not to issue "against the separate property of the infant or against his share, if any, in the partnership profits". The Court of Appeal had rescinded a receiving order because one partner was an infant (In re Beauchamp Brothers [1894] 1 QB 1). In the Lords, Lord Herschell said the judgment "was erroneous and required to be amended" by inserting "other than Gilbert Walter Beauchamp" after "defendant", so that it was "a valid receiving order against Ralph Beauchamp" and the receiver was also appointed over the partnership assets "for the purpose of protecting them for the benefit of the creditors".
  - The Calcutta court read the Lords' decision as no authority that a receiver may reach the infant's other property.
- Use: shows the English position that an infant partner is not personally liable for firm debts and cannot be made bankrupt as a partner. The pack's description ("Not personally liable for firm debts during minority") matches this secondary account. Read the original (ICLR or a library) before quoting Lord Herschell.

### G1. Halbman v Lemke, 99 Wis. 2d 241, 298 N.W.2d 562 (Wisconsin Supreme Court, Callow J, 25 November 1980)
- Question: whether a minor who disaffirms the purchase of a car (not a necessity) must pay for damage before disaffirmance. File: `halbman_cap.txt`. (The pack's date "1980" is right; the CAP record gives 1980-11-25.)
- Holding, verbatim: "we hold that, absent misrepresentation or tortious damage to the property, a minor who disaffirms a contract for the purchase of an item which is not a necessity may recover his purchase price without liability for use, depreciation, damage, or other diminution in value."
- Also: "As a general rule a minor who disaffirms a contract is entitled to recover all consideration he has conferred incident to the transaction ... In return the minor is expected to restore as much of the consideration as, at the time of disaffirmance, remains in the minor's possession." The opinion cites "Restatement (Second) of Contracts, sec. 18B, comment c, (Tent. Draft No. 1, 1964)" - a draft numbering, **not** § 14.
- Dodson (below) quotes Halbman for the purpose of the doctrine: to protect minors "from squandering their wealth through improvident contracts with crafty adults who would take advantage of them in the marketplace."

### G2. Dodson v Shrader, 824 S.W.2d 545 (Tennessee Supreme Court, O'Brien J, 27 January 1992)
- Question: 16-year-old bought a used pick-up for $4,900 cash, truck later failed; minor sought full refund. File: `dodson_cap.txt`.
- Holding, verbatim (the rule the Court states and adopts): "where the minor has not been overreached in any way, and there has been no undue influence, and the contract is a fair and reasonable one, and the minor has actually paid money on the purchase price, and taken and used the article purchased, that he ought not to be permitted to recover the amount actually paid, without allowing the vender of the goods reasonable compensation for the use of, depreciation, and willful or negligent damage to the article purchased, while in his hands."
- Adopted: "we conclude that the rule, as we have indicated, and which we have paraphrased from that adopted in the State of Oregon, will henceforth be the rule to be utilized in this State."
- Rationale on business dealing: "This rule is best adapted to modern conditions under which minors are permitted to, and do in fact, transact a great deal of business for themselves, long before they have reached the age of legal majority." Also describes the split: the "Benefit Rule" and the "use/depreciation" rule as the two minority rules against the traditional full-refund rule.
- Pair Halbman (full refund, subject to misrepresentation or tort) with Dodson (deduction for use/depreciation) as the two poles.

### G3. Bush v Linthicum, 59 Md. 344 (Maryland Court of Appeals, Irving J, 9 February 1883) - minor partner
- Source: `capx_bush_linthicum.json`. The pack had only the record. A minor partner, sued by his adult partner for dissolution, pleaded infancy; the plea was not allowed to defeat the suit where the adult had been deceived about his age.
- Quotations: "although he may be a partner, he cannot be held for contracts of the firm individually unless he affirms, or does that which amounts to affirmance after reaching majority." Citing Michigan authority (Dunton v Brown; Armitage v Widoe): an infant who made a partnership could not disaffirm it until he came of age. "Having formed this partnership, he cannot so far repudiate it during minority, as to escape such consequences of partnership as do not involve personal liability for claims against the firm, or costs incident to the legal settlement of its affairs ... What his rights may be, as against his adult co-partners, when he reaches majority, we do not decide."
- Use: a nineteenth-century US case with the same shape as Partnership Act s. 30 (partner but not personally liable). Old; check later state authority before relying.
